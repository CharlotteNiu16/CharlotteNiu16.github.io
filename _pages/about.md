Hi! I’m **Ruijie Niu**, an M.S. student in **Biostatistics at the University of Pittsburgh School of Public Health**.

My research interests lie at the intersection of **causal inference, clinical biostatistics, and biomedical data science**. I am particularly interested in problems where the scientific question is easy to state but difficult to answer from observed data—and where statistical methods need to be both theoretically defensible and practically useful.

I tend to understand abstract methods best by moving back and forth between **derivations, simulations, and real data**. That has shaped much of my current work: methodological research on causal mediation with negative controls, Bayesian modeling for high-dimensional biomedical data, and collaborative statistical analyses in oncology and pediatric endocrinology.

I care about methodology, but not methodology for its own sake. What keeps me interested is understanding **when a method can answer a real question, when it fails, and why that distinction matters to the people using the result**.

I am currently exploring Ph.D. opportunities beginning in **Fall 2027** in biostatistics and related quantitative health fields.

# 🔬 Current Research

## Causal Mediation with Negative Controls and Difference-in-Differences

*University of Pittsburgh · with Dr. Qiong Wu*

A central challenge in causal mediation analysis is that direct and indirect effects may not be identified when important confounders are unobserved. My current methodological work studies how negative-control information and related difference-in-differences structures can be used to reason about mediation effects under explicit identification assumptions.

Under faculty supervision, I work on **identification derivations, estimator implementation, simulation design, and computational evaluation**. The current implementation uses individual-level plug-in / g-computation estimators with participant-bootstrap inference.

In the latest formal simulation study, I evaluated **27 scenarios across 27,000 Monte Carlo replications**, examining bias, interval coverage, standard-error calibration, negative-control noise and loading mismatch, exposure–mediator interactions, mediator types, and nuisance-model misspecification.

One aspect I find especially interesting is not simply whether an estimator performs well, but **why it succeeds in one data-generating setting and fails in another**. A real-data application is planned as the next stage of the project.

## Bayesian Factor Models for High-Dimensional Biomedical Data

*University of Pittsburgh · with Dr. Qiyiwen Zhang and Dr. Qiong Wu*

I am also studying Bayesian latent-factor methods for high-dimensional biomedical data, with an initial focus on **guided sparse factor analysis (GSFA)** for single-cell perturbation studies.

My current work has emphasized careful reproduction and benchmarking rather than treating an existing method as a black box. I reproduced core GSFA workflows on two single-cell datasets and completed **180 GSFA fits across 90 paired simulation cases**, alongside comparisons with alternative analysis strategies.

The project has made me particularly interested in the gap between a method as described mathematically and its behavior in practice—especially under changes in sparsity, noise, model assumptions, and false-discovery behavior.

This work is serving as a foundation for further methodological exploration.

## Collaborative Clinical Biostatistics

*University of Pittsburgh / UPMC*

Alongside methodological research, I collaborate with clinical investigators in **oncology** and **pediatric endocrinology**.

In oncology projects, I help construct analysis-ready datasets and clinical endpoints and conduct response and time-to-event analyses using methods including Kaplan–Meier estimation, log-rank tests, confidence intervals, and follow-up summaries. I prepare statistical results, figures, and reports for discussion with clinical investigators.

In pediatric endocrinology, I analyze sex differences in cardiometabolic risk following youth-onset type 1 diabetes, including cohort and endpoint construction, attrition and missing-data assessment, logistic regression, and interaction analyses.

These collaborations have reinforced something I value about biostatistics: the statistical problem often begins **before** a model is fitted. Defining the right cohort, outcome, comparison, and interpretation can matter just as much as the eventual model.

## Foundation Models in Emergency and Critical Care

*University of Minnesota · with Dr. Feng Xie*

I contribute to a scoping review examining the clinical utility, decision-making role, implementation, and research gaps of foundation models in emergency and critical care.

The review synthesizes **49 eligible studies** under PRISMA-ScR / JBI guidance and has been submitted to *Critical Care*. My contribution centers on evidence synthesis and manuscript development rather than the training or deployment of foundation models.
